# AI-Native Build-versus-Buy Calculator

## Purpose

Compare buying, assembling, and owning a capability after AI lowers the cost of
implementation. Evaluate the complete operated obligation, not feature parity
or license price alone.

This instrument uses two gates in order:

1. **Fit:** can the option meet every non-negotiable requirement at the required
   consequence and evidence floor?
2. **Ownership:** among the viable options, which lifecycle obligation should
   the organization accept?

Do not let a preference for vendor ownership convert a failed requirement into
a passing one.

An option that fails a non-negotiable row is not admitted to the ownership
comparison. An unknown requires investigation, a bounded pilot, or an explicit
decision not to proceed.

## Candidate options

Describe at least four:

1. continue the current process or do nothing;
2. buy an integrated product;
3. assemble purchased components behind owned contracts;
4. build and operate the capability;
5. replace only a differentiating or expensive module, if relevant.

You may also use **buy to learn** as a time-bounded discovery option. Define its
learning objective, reliance limit, portability boundary, decision date, and
trigger for staying, assembling, replacing, building, or stopping before the
trial begins.

## Seam ledger

For each requirement a purchased or assembled option cannot meet directly,
record the compensation as part of that option rather than treating it as free
internal work.

| Fit gap | Compensation or workaround | Accountable owner | Coupled systems | Vendor-change break trigger | Detection and recovery | Recurring load | Removal path |

If no one owns a required compensation, the option does not pass the
requirements gate.

## Strategic-value worksheet

Score with evidence, not intuition alone:

- Does proprietary workflow or data create differentiation?
- Does ownership shorten the decision and learning cycle?
- Does the vendor pool expertise, certifications, data, networks, or liability
  that would be costly to reproduce?
- Does customization improve customer outcome or merely mirror preference?
- Which option preserves authoritative intent, evidence, and decision history?
- Which dependency gains bargaining power?
- Can the capability survive provider failure or exit?
- What other work will internal ownership displace?

## Replacement-slice experiment

Before a major commitment, choose one representative slice and pre-register:

- equivalent outcome and evidence floor;
- integration boundary;
- failure and recovery test;
- security and privacy review;
- user/adoption measure;
- human effort, elapsed time, and support load;
- observation window;
- conditions favoring buy, assemble, build, or stop.

Do not compare a vendor demonstration with an internal production candidate.
Make both paths satisfy equivalent consequence and evidence requirements.

## Obligation conversion

If the internal experiment succeeds, explicitly decide who funds and owns:

- service objectives and support;
- security monitoring and patching;
- compliance evidence;
- roadmap and compatibility;
- data stewardship;
- disaster recovery;
- migration and retirement.

If those owners do not exist, the organization has proved a prototype, not a
replacement.

## Worked fictional example

Alder & Finch considers replacing a multimillion-dollar workflow product. An
AI-built slice reproduces the visible approval flow quickly and fits local work
better. Equivalent-path testing reveals that migration, regulatory evidence,
after-hours support, and two ecosystem integrations dominate implementation
cost. The company builds the differentiating decision interface, purchases a
commodity records service, retains its decision history, and schedules an exit
exercise. AI did not prove that buying was wasteful; it made a hybrid option
cheap enough to test.
